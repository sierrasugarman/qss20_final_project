Church and State:
Is There an Impact on
Domestic Conflict?

A cross-regional analysis of state-sponsored religion and intrastate violence

Nearly one-third of global governments formally sponsor a state religion — but does this institutional choice make countries more or less prone to internal conflict? This project analyzes conflict deaths across regions and examines whether specific religious characteristics predict violence, finding that the relationship is real but dependent on context.

01   Research Questions

Three Research Questions

Religion is one of the most politically powerful forces in the modern world — yet its relationship to domestic conflict remains underexplored. This project sets out to answer three interconnected questions.

RQ1Is there a correlation between whether a country has a state-sponsored religion and the magnitude of internal conflict it experiences?
RQ2How does the relationship between state-sponsored religion and intrastate conflict vary across regions?
RQ3Which characteristics of religions — collectivism, political theology, exclusivity, hierarchy, pluralism — are associated with higher levels of intrastate conflict?
02   Data

Data

This project combines conflict data with religious and demographic data to build a country-year dataset spanning 35 years and 106 countries.

⚔️

Uppsala Conflict Data Program

Country-year conflict data tracking intrastate battle deaths from 1989 to 2024. Each observation is a country-year with at least one active intrastate conflict.

Primary outcome variable
🏛️

QoG State Religion Dataset

Country-level classification of whether a government officially sponsors a state religion. Treated as time-invariant and merged on country name.

Key explanatory variable
🕐

Pew Research Center Demographics

Country-level religious composition data for 2020, used to identify the majority religion in each country and attach doctrinal trait scores.

Religion characteristics
03   Methods

Methods

After cleaning each dataset independently, the three sources were merged in two sequential left joins anchored to the UCDP violence data. Originally exact string matching was used, but this left 322 observations missing state religion data. Fuzzy string matching (80% similarity threshold) reduced missing classifications from 322 to just 43 — an 87% improvement.

A religion trait table was hand-coded based on the literature, scoring each majority religion on five dimensions on a 1–5 scale:

04   Results

Results

The analysis reveals that the impact of state-sponsored religion on conflict varies by region. While Latin America-Caribbean and Sub-Saharan Africa show more median deaths in countries with no state-sponsored religion, the Middle East-North Africa and Asia-Pacific show more deaths in countries with a state-sponsored religion.

Figure 1Median Intrastate Conflict Deaths by Region and State Religion Type
Median battle-related deaths from intrastate conflict grouped by region and state religion status. Europe and North America were omitted due to insufficient sample size. Source: UCDP; QoG State Religion Dataset.

"The impact of state-sponsored religion on conflict varies by region — it is not a negligible difference, but it is not consistent globally either."

In Figure 1, regional intrastate conflict is represented through median deaths per conflict in countries within each region. While Latin America-Caribbean and Sub-Saharan Africa show more median deaths in countries with no state-sponsored religion, the Middle East-North Africa and Asia-Pacific show more deaths in countries with a state-sponsored religion. One aspect of this variation that is notable is that by region, it is not a negligible difference in whether state-sponsored religion is correlated with high conflict levels. Rather, there is a large difference in conflict levels across regions based on this factor, however it is not consistent globally. One likely cause of this variation are the regional and historic processes that influence conflict. The Middle East and Asia-Pacific possess a unique combination of religion and state authority that creates heightened political instability. When considering the other regions, lack of state-sponsored religion could point to a lack of political unity within a state, spurring domestic conflict.

Figure 2Religion Traits and Intrastate Violence (State-Sponsored Countries Only)
Pearson correlations between five author-coded religion trait scores and log-transformed intrastate conflict deaths, among state-religion countries only. Collectivism and political theology have positive correlations; hierarchy, exclusivity, and pluralism tolerance have negative correlations. Source: UCDP; Pew Research Center; author-coded trait scores.

Figure 2 highlights five core religion traits and shows the correlation between their scores and intrastate conflict deaths transformed by a log operation. While all correlations represent modest relationships rather than strong ones, it is clear that traits impact conflict in different ways. Collectivism has a positive correlation with r = 0.14, suggesting that religions emphasizing group cohesion and collective identity are associated with somewhat higher intrastate conflict deaths in state-religion countries. Political theology shows a moderate positive association with r = 0.10, showing that religions that see themselves closer aligned with government are more prone to conflict, consistent with Philpott (2007). Pluralism tolerance shows the strongest negative association with r = -0.11, indicating that religions more tolerant of other faiths are associated with lower conflict deaths even within state-religion contexts, pointing toward a potential protective effect of religious openness. Hierarchy and exclusivity both show small negative correlations close to zero, suggesting that organizational strictness and doctrinal exclusivity do not independently predict conflict intensity in a meaningful way.

05   Conclusion

What This
Means

Main Takeaway

The goal of this analysis was to examine whether state-sponsored religion is associated with higher intrastate conflict deaths across regions and if characteristics of religions can predict conflict intensity. The results suggest that the relationship between state religion and conflict is more nuanced than might otherwise be expected. When analyzed by region, Latin America-Caribbean and Sub-Saharan Africa show more median deaths in countries with no state-sponsored religion, while the Middle East-North Africa and Asia-Pacific show more deaths in countries with a state-sponsored religion. By characteristic, collectivism and political theology are associated positively with conflict while pluralism tolerance has a negative association.

Limitations

No Causal Claims

This is a descriptive analysis, so causal claims cannot be made. There are likely confounding factors including type of government, wealth of a country, and historical context that all play a role in conflict as well.

Author-Coded Trait Rankings

The characteristic scale rankings are based on literature from the field, but others may have different rankings that affect those results and should be interpreted as such.

Snapshot in Time

Because religions and demographics within countries are constantly shifting, it is important to understand these data points as a snapshot in time rather than a final assessment of each country.

Modest Correlations

While associations were present, the strength of the trait correlations were modest and only represent some of the explanation for conflict deaths. The strength of this association should be interpreted as such.
