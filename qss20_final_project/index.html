Church and State:
Is There an Impact on
Domestic Conflict?

A cross-regional analysis of state-sponsored religion and intrastate violence
Sierra Sugarman  ·  June 2026  ·  Uppsala Conflict Data Program + Pew Research Center

Nearly one-third of global governments formally sponsor a state religion — but does this institutional choice make countries more or less prone to internal conflict? This project analyzes conflict deaths across regions and examines whether specific religious characteristics predict violence, finding that the relationship is real but highly conditional on context.

01   Research Questions

Three Questions
Worth Asking

Religion is one of the most politically powerful forces in the modern world — yet its relationship to domestic conflict remains underexplored. This project sets out to answer three interconnected questions.

RQ1Is there a correlation between whether a country has a state-sponsored religion and the magnitude of internal conflict it experiences?
RQ2How does the relationship between state-sponsored religion and intrastate conflict vary across regions?
RQ3Which characteristics of religions — collectivism, political theology, exclusivity, hierarchy, pluralism — are associated with higher levels of intrastate conflict?

03   Methods

From Raw Data
to Analysis

After cleaning each dataset independently, the three sources were merged in two sequential left joins anchored to the UCDP violence data. Originally exact string matching was used, but this left 322 observations missing state religion data. Fuzzy string matching (80% similarity threshold) reduced missing classifications from 322 to just 43 — an 87% improvement.

A religion trait table was hand-coded based on the literature, scoring each majority religion on five dimensions on a 1–5 scale:

04   Results

What the
Data Shows

The analysis reveals that the relationship between state-sponsored religion and intrastate conflict is real but highly conditional — shaped far more by regional context than by a universal pattern.

Figure 1Median Intrastate Conflict Deaths by Region and State Religion Type
Median battle-related deaths from intrastate conflict grouped by region and state religion status. The Middle East–North Africa is the only region where state-religion countries show higher deaths. Three of four regions show the opposite pattern. Source: UCDP; QoG State Religion Dataset.

“The Middle East–North Africa is the only region where state religion correlates with more conflict deaths — in three other regions, the relationship reverses.”

In the Middle East–North Africa, state-religion countries record approximately 245 median deaths compared to 113 for secular states — a twofold difference. But in Asia-Pacific (~205 vs 118), Latin America–Caribbean (~94 vs 75), and Sub-Saharan Africa (~133 vs 12), countries without a state religion actually record higher median deaths. This suggests that prior literature linking state religion to violence may be disproportionately driven by Middle Eastern dynamics.

Figure 2Religion Traits and Intrastate Violence (State-Sponsored Countries Only)
Pearson correlations between five author-coded religion trait scores and log-transformed intrastate conflict deaths, among state-religion countries only. Collectivism (r=0.148) and political theology (r=0.115) show the strongest positive associations. Pluralism tolerance (r=−0.089) shows a negative association. Source: UCDP; Pew Research Center; author-coded trait scores.

Among state-religion countries, collectivism (r = 0.148) and political theology (r = 0.115) show the strongest positive associations with conflict deaths, while pluralism tolerance (r = −0.089) shows a negative association — suggesting religious openness may have a small protective effect. Hierarchy and exclusivity show near-zero correlations.

05   Conclusion

What This
Means

Main Takeaway

State-sponsored religion and intrastate violence are correlated, but the relationship depends heavily on regional and historical context. The Middle East is the exception, not the rule — three of four regions studied show secular states with higher conflict deaths. At the doctrinal level, what kind of religion a state sponsors — particularly how collectively and politically oriented it is — may matter as much as whether it sponsors one at all. These findings are descriptive and correlational; future work should control for confounders like regime type, GDP, and colonial history.

Limitations

⚠ No Causal Claims

This is a descriptive analysis. Many confounders — regime type, GDP, ethnic fractionalization, colonial history — are not controlled for.

⚠ Author-Coded Traits

Religion trait scores are coded by the author based on literature, not drawn from a validated external scale. Different scoring choices could shift results.

⚠ Static Classifications

State religion status and majority religion are treated as fixed, but the conflict data spans 35 years during which both may have changed.

⚠ Remaining Missingness

43 observations (3%) remain without a state religion classification even after fuzzy matching. Systematic missingness could bias results.
